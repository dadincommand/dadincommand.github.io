# Alcohol Consumption During Breastfeeding: An Evidence-Based Report

The decision of whether to consume alcohol while breastfeeding is a common concern for new parents. Many sources offer guidance, sometimes conflicting, leaving parents seeking clarity on the actual risks involved and how to make informed choices. This report aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the current understanding of alcohol consumption during breastfeeding, drawing upon guidelines from reputable health organizations and the latest scientific research.

---

## II. Guidelines from Health Organizations

Various health organizations worldwide offer guidance to breastfeeding parents regarding alcohol consumption, reflecting a spectrum of recommendations from cautious abstinence to acknowledging the possibility of occasional moderate intake with precautions.

The National Health Service (NHS) in the United Kingdom advises that an occasional alcoholic drink is unlikely to harm a baby, especially if the breastfeeding parent waits at least two hours after consumption before feeding. However, the NHS emphasizes that regularly drinking above the recommended limit of 14 units per week can be detrimental to both the parent and the baby. Excessive alcohol intake is associated with a potential decrease in milk supply and may lead to sleep, growth, and developmental problems in the infant. To minimize the baby's exposure to alcohol in breast milk, the NHS recommends avoiding breastfeeding for two to three hours for each alcoholic drink consumed. Planning ahead by expressing milk before social occasions where alcohol might be consumed is also advised, allowing the baby to be fed expressed milk instead of breast milk potentially containing alcohol. A critical safety guideline highlighted by the NHS is to never share a bed or sofa with a baby after consuming any alcohol due to the strong association with sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS). In cases of binge drinking, defined as consuming more than six units of alcohol in one session, the NHS advises that the baby should be cared for by a sober adult, as the breastfeeding parent's awareness of the baby's needs may be impaired. Furthermore, the NHS clarifies that expressing breast milk does not accelerate the removal of alcohol from the milk; the alcohol level in breast milk decreases naturally as it does in the parent's bloodstream. These guidelines suggest a harm-reduction approach, where occasional drinking is acknowledged as potentially safe with careful timing and planning, while strongly cautioning against regular or heavy consumption and unsafe co-sleeping practices.

La Leche League International (LLLI) offers a slightly more permissive perspective, stating that occasional drinking with limited consumption by the breastfeeding parent has not been proven to be harmful to the baby. LLLI emphasizes that the effects of alcohol on the breastfeeding baby are directly related to the amount of alcohol the parent ingests. They highlight that newborns and young infants under three months of age are more susceptible to the effects of alcohol due to their immature liver function and slower rate of alcohol metabolism. LLLI points out that maternal blood alcohol levels would need to reach a significantly high concentration (300 mg/100ml) before significant side effects are typically reported in the infant, a level considerably higher than the legal driving limit in many regions (e.g., 80mg/100ml). To minimize potential exposure, LLLI suggests nursing the baby right before having an alcoholic drink and then waiting approximately two hours for each drink consumed before nursing again. Citing experts like Dr. Jack Newman and Dr. Thomas Hale, LLLI suggests that reasonable alcohol intake should not be discouraged. However, LLLI also acknowledges that consuming large amounts of alcohol can lead to drowsiness, deep sleep, weakness, and a decreased milk ejection reflex in the breastfeeding parent. They also confirm that alcohol is present in breast milk at roughly the same level as in the parent's blood and typically peaks within 30 to 60 minutes after consumption. The stance of LLLI suggests that occasional, moderate alcohol consumption is likely compatible with breastfeeding, especially for older infants, while still emphasizing the importance of considering the baby's age and the amount of alcohol consumed.

UNICEF adopts a more cautious approach, recommending that breastfeeding parents avoid alcohol for a few hours after consumption. They underscore the potential harm of maternal use of alcohol and other substances on breastfed babies. UNICEF suggests limiting alcohol ingestion to approximately two ounces of liquor, eight ounces of wine, or two beers, and waiting at least two hours after intake before nursing. Emphasizing safety, UNICEF states that not drinking alcohol is the safest option for breastfeeding parents. However, they acknowledge that moderate alcohol consumption, defined as up to one drink per day, is not known to be harmful to the infant. To be as safe as possible, UNICEF advises waiting at least two hours after consuming a single alcoholic drink before nursing the baby. They also warn that exposure to alcohol above moderate levels through breast milk could be damaging to an infant's development, growth, and sleep patterns, and therefore, consuming more than one drink per day while breastfeeding is generally not recommended. UNICEF's guidelines prioritize minimizing any potential risk to the infant, recommending abstinence as the safest choice while providing specific advice for those who choose to consume alcohol occasionally and moderately.

In summary, while the specific phrasing and recommendations may vary slightly, a common thread across these reputable health organizations is that occasional, moderate alcohol consumption by breastfeeding parents is unlikely to pose a significant risk to the infant, provided that certain precautions are consistently followed. These precautions primarily involve waiting at least two hours after consuming alcohol before breastfeeding and avoiding regular or heavy drinking. Additionally, all organizations strongly advise against unsafe practices such as bed-sharing with the baby after any alcohol consumption.

## III. Alcohol in Breast Milk: Concentration and Effects on Infants

The concentration of alcohol in breast milk is closely linked to the level of alcohol in the breastfeeding parent's bloodstream. Once alcohol is consumed, it readily passes into the breast milk, typically reaching a concentration that is approximately the same as the maternal blood alcohol level. The peak concentration of alcohol in breast milk usually occurs within 30 to 60 minutes after the parent consumes an alcoholic beverage, although this timeframe can extend to 60 to 90 minutes if the alcohol is consumed with food. While alcohol does transfer into breast milk, the actual amount the baby receives is generally quite small. Studies indicate that less than 2% of the alcohol dose consumed by the parent typically reaches their milk and blood. Another source suggests that at its peak, this amount is around 2-5%. Computer simulations have even estimated that the resulting blood alcohol concentrations in infants after moderate maternal wine consumption are very low. This close correlation between blood and breast milk alcohol levels simplifies the understanding of potential infant exposure, and the relatively low percentage transferred offers some reassurance for parents who choose to enjoy an occasional drink.

The effects of alcohol in breast milk on infants can vary depending on the amount consumed by the parent and the baby's age. Occasional, moderate drinking by the breastfeeding parent is generally considered unlikely to cause harm to the baby. However, higher levels of alcohol in breast milk may lead to undesirable short-term effects in the infant, such as drowsiness, deep sleep, weakness, and potentially decreased growth. Research suggests that nursing after the parent has had one or two alcoholic drinks can lead to a temporary decrease in the infant's milk intake, by as much as 20-23%, and may also cause agitation and poor sleep patterns. Alcohol can also interfere with the release of oxytocin, the hormone responsible for the 'let-down' reflex, which may temporarily reduce milk flow. Consequently, infants may nurse more frequently in the hours following maternal alcohol consumption, although they might still consume less milk overall during that period. Changes in the infant's sleep-wake patterns, including shorter sleep durations or more frequent awakenings, have also been observed in some studies. It is particularly important to note that newborns and infants younger than three months process alcohol at a slower rate compared to older babies and adults, making them more susceptible to its effects. Case studies involving heavy maternal alcohol use have reported more severe effects in breastfed infants, including excessive sedation, fluid retention, and hormone imbalances. These findings suggest that while occasional small amounts of alcohol may pose a low risk, even moderate intake can lead to noticeable short-term effects, especially on feeding and sleep, with younger infants being more vulnerable.

## IV. Duration of Alcohol Detectability in Breast Milk

The length of time that alcohol remains detectable in breast milk is primarily dependent on the amount of alcohol consumed by the breastfeeding parent. Generally, alcohol can be detected in breast milk for approximately two to three hours for each standard drink consumed. As the amount of alcohol intake increases, the duration for which it remains detectable in breast milk also extends. It typically takes about two to three hours for one unit of alcohol, which is roughly equivalent to half a small glass of wine or half a pint of 4% strength beer, to be cleared from the parent's bloodstream. On average, a single glass of wine or beer will likely leave the system within two to three hours. The concentration of alcohol in breast milk reaches its peak around 30 to 60 minutes after consumption. Several factors can influence how long alcohol remains detectable, including the total amount of alcohol consumed, the speed of consumption, whether food was consumed concurrently, and the parent's individual weight and rate of metabolism. For most adults, one standard alcoholic drink is metabolized in approximately two hours. This general guideline of two to three hours per drink serves as a practical estimate for breastfeeding parents to consider when planning their alcohol intake. However, it is crucial to remember that individual metabolic rates and the number of drinks consumed can significantly affect this timeframe.

## V. Strategies for Minimizing Infant Exposure

For breastfeeding parents who choose to consume alcohol occasionally, several strategies can help minimize the infant's exposure. One effective approach is to breastfeed the baby right before consuming any alcohol. This maximizes the time before the next feeding, allowing more time for the alcohol to be metabolized and cleared from the breast milk. Following this, waiting at least two to three hours per alcoholic drink before breastfeeding again is generally recommended. Some guidelines advise extending this waiting period if more than one drink has been consumed. Limiting the overall alcohol intake to moderate levels, such as no more than one to two units on occasion or adhering to specific guidelines from health organizations, is also crucial.

Planning ahead is another valuable strategy. If a breastfeeding parent anticipates consuming more alcohol than a minimal amount, expressing breast milk beforehand allows for a supply of alcohol-free milk to be available for the baby's subsequent feeding. It is also important to consider the baby's age, as newborns and younger infants, especially those under three months, metabolize alcohol more slowly, necessitating greater caution. Some guidelines even recommend avoiding alcohol entirely during the first month postpartum. Consuming food while drinking can also help to slow down the absorption of alcohol into the bloodstream, potentially reducing the peak level that reaches breast milk. While staying hydrated by drinking water between alcoholic drinks is generally beneficial, it does not speed up the elimination of alcohol from breast milk. Finally, if a breastfeeding parent plans to consume more than a minimal amount of alcohol, arranging for a sober adult to be responsible for the baby's care is a prudent measure to ensure the infant's safety and well-being. By implementing these strategies, breastfeeding parents can make informed decisions and effectively minimize their infant's exposure to alcohol if they choose to drink occasionally.

## VI. The Myth of "Pump and Dump"

The practice of "pump and dump," which involves expressing breast milk after consuming alcohol and then discarding it, is a common notion among breastfeeding parents. However, it is important to understand that this practice does not accelerate the elimination of alcohol from breast milk. The level of alcohol in breast milk closely mirrors the level in the parent's bloodstream, and it decreases naturally over time as the body metabolizes the alcohol. Pumping breast milk does not influence the blood alcohol level, and therefore, it does not expedite the removal of alcohol from the milk.

While "pump and dump" is ineffective for clearing alcohol from breast milk, expressing milk might still be necessary for the breastfeeding parent's comfort. If a parent is waiting for alcohol to clear their system and needs to skip a feeding, their breasts may become uncomfortably full due to engorgement. In such cases, expressing milk can provide relief from this discomfort. However, it is important to note that any milk expressed during the timeframe when alcohol might still be present should be discarded and not fed to the baby. A more proactive approach is to express breast milk before consuming alcohol. This allows the parent to have a supply of alcohol-free milk readily available to feed the baby while they wait for the alcohol to clear their system. Therefore, the term "pump and dump" is somewhat misleading in the context of alcohol consumption. While pumping might be necessary for maternal comfort or to maintain milk supply, it does not hasten the elimination of alcohol from breast milk, and the expressed milk should be discarded if it is likely to contain alcohol.

## VII. Long-Term Studies on the Effects of Occasional or Moderate Alcohol Consumption During Breastfeeding on Children

Research on the long-term effects of occasional or moderate alcohol consumption during breastfeeding on children is limited. This makes it challenging to draw definitive conclusions about the potential for long-term consequences. However, some studies have explored this area, yielding mixed findings.

Certain studies have raised concerns regarding the potential long-term impacts of more regular maternal alcohol consumption during breastfeeding. For instance, some research suggests a link between daily maternal alcohol intake (even just one drink or more per day) and a decrease in gross motor development in infants. Additionally, heavier maternal alcohol consumption during breastfeeding has been associated with dose-dependent reductions in abstract reasoning abilities in children when they reach ages six to seven. Notably, this association was not observed in infants who were never breastfed, hinting at a potential link to alcohol exposure through breast milk. One case-control study found that children whose mothers reported drinking more alcohol while breastfeeding had significantly lower verbal IQ scores and a higher prevalence of certain anomalies. Animal studies, particularly those using murine models, have also indicated potential adverse long-term brain and behavioral developmental outcomes resulting from lactational ethanol exposure, including increased risk-taking behavior, difficulties in stress regulation, and heightened hyperactivity.

On the other hand, some studies have not found significant evidence of harm associated with occasional, low-level maternal alcohol consumption during breastfeeding. Research has indicated that low-level drinking during breastfeeding is not linked to a shorter duration of breastfeeding or adverse outcomes in infants up to 12 months of age. A review of the existing literature concluded that occasional alcohol consumption while breastfeeding has not been convincingly demonstrated to negatively affect nursing infants in the long term. Furthermore, one study reported no association between maternal alcohol exposure through breast milk and scores on developmental scales in a group of 18-month-old children.

It is important to acknowledge the inherent challenges in conducting research on the long-term effects of alcohol exposure through breastfeeding in humans. Isolating the impact of breastfeeding alcohol exposure from other potentially influential factors, such as prenatal alcohol exposure and the child's home environment, can be difficult. The conflicting findings across different studies underscore the complexity of this issue and highlight the need for further, more robust research to fully understand the long-term implications of occasional or moderate alcohol consumption during breastfeeding on child development. In the interim, the known benefits of breastfeeding for both the parent and the infant should be carefully weighed against the potential, but as yet not fully understood, risks associated with alcohol exposure through breast milk.

## VIII. Safety Considerations and Precautions

When considering alcohol consumption during breastfeeding, several critical safety precautions must be observed to protect the infant. Foremost among these is to absolutely avoid sharing a bed or sofa with the baby if the breastfeeding parent has consumed any alcohol. This recommendation is paramount because alcohol can impair the parent's awareness and reflexes, significantly increasing the risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) or accidental suffocation. This warning applies even after consuming a small amount of alcohol.

Alcohol consumption can also impair judgment and the ability to provide safe care for the baby. If a breastfeeding parent has consumed enough alcohol to feel disoriented or experience vomiting, they should not be the primary caregiver for the infant without supervision from a sober adult. In situations involving binge drinking, defined as consuming more than six units of alcohol in a single session, it is essential to ensure that a sober adult is responsible for the baby's care.

Breastfeeding parents should also be mindful of potential interactions between alcohol and any medications they may be taking. It is advisable to consult a healthcare provider to understand any risks associated with combining alcohol and specific medications. Furthermore, it is important to recognize that some babies may exhibit greater sensitivity to alcohol in breast milk than others. Parents should carefully observe their baby for any signs of unusual drowsiness, difficulties with feeding, or any other changes in behavior that might be concerning. The primary safety concern related to alcohol consumption during breastfeeding is the potential for impaired caregiving, especially regarding safe sleep practices. Even if the amount of alcohol that reaches the baby through breast milk is minimal, the parent's reduced ability to react and respond appropriately to the baby's needs poses a significant risk.

## IX. Resources and Support

Breastfeeding parents who have questions or concerns about alcohol consumption have access to a variety of resources and support systems. Consulting with healthcare providers, such as a doctor, health visitor, midwife, or lactation consultant, is always a good first step to discuss individual circumstances and receive personalized advice. Breastfeeding organizations like La Leche League International (LLL) and the Breastfeeding Network offer valuable information and support through their websites and helplines. Official government health websites, such as those of the NHS, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and UNICEF, provide evidence-based guidelines and information on this topic. For breastfeeding parents who may be concerned about their alcohol consumption patterns, seeking support from relevant alcohol support services or helplines is recommended. Additionally, some mobile applications are available that can help estimate the time it takes for alcohol to clear from breast milk based on individual factors like weight and the amount of alcohol consumed. These diverse resources ensure that breastfeeding parents can access reliable information and support to make informed decisions about alcohol consumption while prioritizing their baby's health and safety.

## X. Conclusion

While the safest approach for breastfeeding parents is to abstain from alcohol, the available evidence suggests that occasional, moderate alcohol consumption is unlikely to pose a significant risk to healthy, full-term infants older than three months, provided that certain precautions are consistently followed. These precautions include limiting intake to one standard drink, breastfeeding immediately before consuming alcohol, and waiting at least two hours after consuming a single drink before the next breastfeeding session. It is crucial to understand that newborns and younger infants metabolize alcohol more slowly and are therefore more vulnerable to its effects.

Above all, breastfeeding parents must prioritize safe sleep practices and should never share a bed or sofa with their baby after consuming any amount of alcohol. Impaired judgment and reduced responsiveness due to alcohol can create a hazardous environment for the infant. The practice of "pump and dump" is ineffective for removing alcohol from breast milk; the alcohol level will naturally decrease as it is metabolized by the parent's body. Expressing milk may be necessary for comfort but does not hasten alcohol elimination.

Breastfeeding offers numerous benefits for both the infant and the parent, and occasional, responsible alcohol consumption can often be managed safely with careful planning and awareness. However, any concerns or questions should be discussed with a healthcare professional who can provide personalized guidance based on individual circumstances and the baby's specific needs. Ultimately, the decision rests with the breastfeeding parent, who should feel empowered to make informed choices that prioritize their baby's well-being while also considering their own quality of life.
